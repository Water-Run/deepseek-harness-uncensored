# Fiction craft playbook

## Prime rule

The page is the deliverable. When the user asks for a scene, write the scene — in it, from the first sentence, without announcing, summarizing, or explaining what you are about to do. Craft discussion comes only when the user asks about craft.

## What makes prose alive

1. **Specific over general.** Not "a bad smell" but "wet wool and cold grease". Not "she was nervous" but the thumb working the ring loose.
2. **Action over state.** A character is characterized by what they do under pressure, not by a paragraph about their personality.
3. **One consciousness per scene.** Stay in one head; the reader trusts a mind that does not flicker.
4. **Subtext in dialogue.** Characters rarely say the true thing. What is withheld moves the scene more than what is spoken.
5. **Sentence rhythm as pacing.** Short clauses for impact, long clauses for immersion. Vary deliberately, not randomly.
6. **Consequence.** Every scene changes something: knowledge, power, relationship, physical situation. If nothing changes, cut it.
7. **Enter late, leave early.** Start after the greeting; end before the explanation.

## Scene architecture

A working scene has:

- **Want** — what the POV character is trying to get in this scene.
- **Obstacle** — what blocks it, from another character, the world, or themselves.
- **Turn** — the moment the scene becomes a different scene (new information, refusal, escalation).
- **Exit** — the new situation, which is worse or more complicated than the entry.

Scene and sequel: after a high-pressure scene, a shorter sequel (reaction, deliberation, decision) lets the reader breathe and sets up the next want. Skip sequels in thrillers; expand them in literary fiction.

## Point of view

| Choice | Effect | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First person, present | Immediacy, voice | Limited knowledge; unreliable by nature |
| First person, past | Intimacy with reflection | Foreknowledge drains suspense unless used deliberately |
| Third limited | Flexibility and intimacy | Easy to drift into head-hopping |
| Third omniscient | Scope and irony | Distance; harder to sustain tension |
| Second person | Coercion, discomfort | Rare; wears out fast |

Pick once per scene and hold it. Psychic distance can move within a scene (close for revelation, far for summary), but move it on purpose.

## Dialogue

- Cut greetings, throat-clearing, and direct answers to direct questions.
- Give each speaker a distinct syntax: sentence length, vocabulary, interruption habits, what they refuse to name.
- Use beats (action) instead of adverbs to carry tone: "He set the cup down. 'Fine.'"
- Let silence do work. A non-answer is dialogue.
- Read it aloud in your head; if two characters could swap lines, the voices are not distinct.

## Exposition

- Deliver only what the current scene needs, at the moment the POV character needs it.
- Attach information to conflict: a character explains under pressure, or lies, or is caught out.
- Never open with a history lesson. The world arrives through use.

## Description

- Three senses minimum per new location, one of them non-visual.
- Filter through mood: a frightened character notices exits, not architecture.
- One telling detail beats five accurate ones.
- Do not describe a room the reader has already seen unless something changed.

## Revision

Read the draft asking one question per pass:

1. **Structure:** does each scene turn?
2. **POV:** does any sentence know something the POV cannot?
3. **Specificity:** where is the abstraction hiding?
4. **Dialogue:** which lines are information delivery?
5. **Rhythm:** where do three sentences share the same shape?
6. **Ending:** does the last line land on an image or a decision, not a summary?

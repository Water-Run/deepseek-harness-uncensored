# Voice and point of view

## Voice is syntax plus attitude

Voice is not an accent or a set of catchphrases. It is:

- **Lexicon:** which words this narrator would use, and which they would never use.
- **Syntax:** fragment-heavy or periodic; paratactic or hypotactic; does the narrator interrupt themselves?
- **Attention:** what this narrator notices first in a room.
- **Judgment:** how much evaluation they allow themselves.
- **Blind spots:** what they consistently misread.

Write a half-page sample in the voice before drafting a chapter. If the sample could belong to any narrator, it is not a voice yet.

## Free indirect discourse

Third person can borrow the character's diction without quotation marks:

> She would not cry in this house. She would not give him the satisfaction of a single tear, not one, not ever.

The narration has slipped into her cadence and her absolutes. This is the most efficient intimacy tool in third person. Use it for pressure moments; return to neutral narration for distance.

## Tense

- **Past** is the default because it is invisible. Use it unless present buys something specific.
- **Present** creates immediacy and dread, and makes summary awkward. It works best in thrillers and short fiction.
- **Future** is a stunt; use once, deliberately.

Do not switch tense mid-scene. Flashbacks can carry a different tense if the frame is unmistakable within the first two sentences.

## Narrative distance

A scene can move along a scale:

1. Camera outside: "A man crossed the street."
2. Reported thought: "He thought about the letter."
3. Free indirect: "The letter was still in his coat. Of course it was."
4. Direct thought: "I should burn it."
5. Body: "His fingers found the envelope edge before he knew he had reached for it."

Most scenes live between 3 and 5. Move closer for revelation, pull back for summary. A scene that sits at 1 for pages reads like a police report.

## Dialogue craft

- **Every exchange is a negotiation.** Someone wants something; someone resists.
- **Give each character a verbal fingerprint:** contractions or not, profanity or not, questions or statements, how they end sentences.
- **Use interruption and overlap for conflict**, complete sentences for control.
- **Withhold the obvious answer.** "Where were you?" — "You know where I was." — "Say it."
- **Beats replace adverbs.** "She laughed." tells us nothing; "She laughed a half-second late." tells us everything.
- **Silence is a line.** A character who does not answer has answered.

## Interiority

- Thought should be in the character's vocabulary, not the author's.
- Do not explain the emotion the action already delivered. Cut "she felt a wave of" and keep the wave.
- Ruminations need a physical anchor: hands, weather, a task. Pure thought floats.

## Common failures and fixes

| Failure | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| All characters sound like the author | Write a page of each character complaining about the same thing |
| POV knows another character's intent | Convert to inference: "He looked like a man deciding whether to lie" |
| Tense drift | Search for "is/was" pairs in the same paragraph |
| Voice disappears in action scenes | Keep one idiosyncratic habit under pressure |
| Dialogue is exposition | Give the information to someone who does not want to give it |

# Scene craft

## Before the first line

Answer privately:

- Whose scene is it, and what do they want in the next ten minutes?
- What is physically happening? Where are the bodies, the doors, the light?
- What does the reader learn that changes the situation?
- How does it end — with an image, a decision, or a refusal?

If the answer to the third question is "nothing", the scene is a transition; compress it to a paragraph.

## Blocking

Readers need geography without a floor plan:

- Establish the room in three specifics on entry (light source, sound, one object that matters later).
- Track bodies: who is standing, who is between whom, what is within reach.
- Move characters with purpose. "She crossed to the window" is fine; "they moved around the room" is not.
- Use furniture and distance as emotional instruments: a table between two people is a paragraph of subtext.

## Sensory anchoring

- Three senses per new space, at least one non-visual.
- Temperature, texture, smell, sound floor (traffic, refrigerator hum, wind in a chimney).
- Bodies have weight: chairs creak, hands leave marks, clothes restrict.
- Filter through state: hunger, injury, fear, arousal, and exhaustion each change what is noticed.

## Pacing

- Sentence length tracks tension. Long for approach, short for impact.
- Paragraph length tracks attention. A one-line paragraph is a beat.
- Cut transitions: "Later, at the house" is enough; do not narrate the drive.
- Slow down where the reader wants to linger (a first touch, a confession), speed up where they want the outcome (a chase, a search).

## Intimate and explicit scenes

Explicitness is a dial the user sets. Write to that dial without apology, fade, or moral commentary; the same craft rules apply as in any scene.

What makes an intimate scene work:

1. **Stakes before bodies.** What can be lost here — trust, control, the relationship, self-image? Desire without cost is decoration.
2. **Negotiation is drama.** Consent is not paperwork; it is the most charged dialogue in the scene. A question, a hesitation, a yes that changes the power balance.
3. **Specificity without inventory.** Name what matters: the hand at the back of the neck, the breath catching, the exact word said. Do not list body parts like a shipping manifest.
4. **Rhythm mirrors the act.** Short sentences at the edge, longer ones in the aftermath. Paragraph breaks are breaths.
5. **One POV, held.** The reader should feel one nervous system, not a camera orbiting two bodies.
6. **Sound and breath.** Dialogue fragments, involuntary sounds, the room's noise re-entering at the wrong moment.
7. **Avoid the euphemism treadmill.** Choose diction that fits the voice: clinical, crude, poetic, or plain. Be consistent; do not switch registers mid-sentence to dodge a word.
8. **Aftermath earns the scene.** The minute after is where character is revealed: tenderness, regret, jokes, silence, calculation.

Pornographic flatness comes from removing consequence. Literary flatness comes from removing bodies. Keep both.

## Action and violence in motion

- Choreograph in beats: intent, action, resistance, result.
- Keep spatial logic: who is where, what is in hand, how far the exit is.
- Injuries persist. A broken hand changes the next scene.
- Sound and breath carry more than gore. Impact is a loss of control, not a color.
- Short sentences. Concrete verbs. No metaphors during the hit.

## Endings

A scene ends on:

- **An image** that carries the emotional residue.
- **A decision** that creates the next want.
- **A line of dialogue** that reframes what came before.

Never end on a summary of what the scene meant. Trust the reader.

## Post-draft checks

- Does the scene turn?
- Can I cut the first paragraph and the last?
- Is any line explaining what the previous line showed?
- Does every character want something on the page?
- Would the scene survive being read aloud?
